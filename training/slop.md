# slop.md

> **This is a RUNNING list.** Add every offense you catch, the moment you catch it — a phrase, a construction, a move, anything that made you wince in a draft. When sweeping a draft against this list, **loose-match variants**: "game-changing," "a real game changer," and "changes the game" are all the same offense. Match the move, not the string.
>
> Below are eleven phrase-level offenses to start with, plus a section on word mosaics — the sentence-level register that produces most of them. Delete the ones you don't personally hate — a rule you don't believe in won't survive contact with a deadline. The starter list is the least valuable thing in this file. **Half the value is that the list is yours:** it becomes the running record of everything your drafting process has ever gotten wrong on you, and each entry is a mistake it will never make again.

## The starting eleven

**"game-changer"** (and game-changing, changes the game)
Kill it by: stating the actual change. What is different now that wasn't before? Write that instead.

**"unlock"** (unlocking, unleash, supercharge)
Kill it by: naming the thing that becomes possible, in plain verbs. "You can now send without copying it over by hand."

**"Let's dive in"** (and buckle up, let's get into it, without further ado)
Kill it by: deleting the sentence. It's throat-clearing before the piece starts. Open on the first real thing you have to say.

**"In today's fast-paced world of…"** (in an era where, in the age of, now more than ever)
Kill it by: deleting the entire opening paragraph and starting at paragraph two — which is almost always where the piece actually begins.

**"It's not just X — it's Y"** (more than a Z, it's a W)
Kill it by: cutting straight to Y. The X half only exists to manufacture a reveal that isn't one.

**"elevate"** (and empower, transform, revolutionize)
Kill it by: replacing with the specific, smaller, truer verb. Usually "improve," often "change," sometimes nothing at all.

**"seamless"** (and effortless, frictionless, streamlined)
Kill it by: describing the friction that's gone. "No copy-paste step" beats "seamless integration" every time.

**"delve"** (and explore, uncover, embark on a journey, navigate the landscape)
Kill it by: using the verb for what you're actually doing — look at, test, compare, read. If it's a transition and not an action, cut it.

**The moralizing wrap-up paragraph** (the closer that restates the piece and tells the reader what it all means for them)
Kill it by: deleting the last paragraph. Check whether the piece is now better. It usually is. If you need a closer, make it one concrete instruction or one image, not a summary with a lesson attached.

**The relentless rule-of-three** (every list is three items, every sentence has three adjectives, everything is "faster, cheaper, better")
Kill it by: breaking the pattern on purpose. Some lists are two things. Some are five. When a triad shows up, check whether the third item is real or was added for rhythm — cut it if it was.

**The paraprosdokian** (the sentence that sets you up, then swerves at the end so you have to re-read the first half as a joke)
"It worked perfectly, right up until someone used it." "We shipped on time, on budget, and to nobody." "The meeting went great; everyone agreed to have another meeting." "I've done this for twenty years, which is how I know I'm still guessing." One is a wink. AI drafts do it every third paragraph, and the reader stops trusting any sentence to mean what it says.
Kill it by: writing the sentence in the order you'd say it to someone across a table. If the swerve is the actual point, lead with it and cut the setup. If it's a joke, ask whether it's earned or whether the writer is just being clever at the reader's expense, and cut it if it's the second one.

## Word mosaics

From Mike Caulfield's ["My de-Claudifying memory file"](https://mikecaulfield.substack.com/p/my-de-claudifying-memory-file-to). A word mosaic is prose in the poetic-compression register — it reads like a collage of impressive fragments while hiding the agents, the objects, and the causal structure, so contradictory readings can coexist. His example: "returns home wearing his blood and a self-defense story" instead of "returns home covered in his blood, and tells the police that she killed him in self-defense." In a proposal or PRD, a sentence with two possible readings isn't style — it's a future scope dispute. The umbrella rule, per Caulfield: **write concretely — explicit subjects and objects, explicit causal relations, no zeugmas, no metaphorical/poetic/analogical language.** The entries below are its enforceable parts.

**The hidden agent** (possessives and passives with no owner: "the migration's risks," "concerns were raised," "the data tells a story")
Who owns it? Who raised it? Who did what to what?
Kill it by: naming the agent and giving the verb a subject and a direct object. "The finance team raised two concerns about the migration."

**The zeugma** (one verb straining to serve two unlike objects: "wearing his blood and a self-defense story," "managing expectations and a Postgres cluster")
It reads as clever and compresses two claims into one unverifiable blur.
Kill it by: giving each verb its own object, even if that means two sentences. Say the first thing, then say the second thing.

**Implied causality** (clauses set side by side so the reader infers the connection: "The report ran overnight. Adoption stalled.")
Juxtaposition lets the writer suggest a causal link without committing to one — which means it can't be checked or disputed.
Kill it by: connecting the clauses with the word that states the actual relation — because, so, when, after, but, despite. If you can't pick one, you don't yet know the relation, and the sentence is hiding that.

**The metaphor doing a claim's job** ("the mode steps up and never falls back," "the pipeline groans under load," "the dashboard becomes the team's heartbeat")
A literal claim can be wrong; a metaphor can only be vibed with. Analytical and client-facing prose needs claims.
Kill it by: writing the literal version and checking whether it's still true. "These reveals increase in the 1970s and don't decline." If the literal version is boring, the metaphor was hiding that it's also thin.

**The floating statistic** ("23% saw improvement," "adoption doubled")
Of what? From what baseline? Over what period?
Kill it by: stating the denominator and the baseline in the sentence itself. "23% of the twist films we catalogued." If you don't know the denominator, the number doesn't go in the document.

**The mosaic test** (run on any sentence that feels impressive)
Ask: are there hidden subjects, objects, or subordinate relations? Could two readers walk away with contradictory readings and both feel confirmed? If yes to either, it's a mosaic.
Kill it by: rewriting as who-did-what-to-what-and-why, then adding style back only if the claims survive.
